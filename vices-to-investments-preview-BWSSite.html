BEFORE WE START

Preface

Let me ask you something, and I want you to answer it honestly, just for yourself. How much did you spend last month on things you didn't need?

I don't mean the rent or the light bill. I mean the extras: the smoke, the drinks, the late-night food, the bets, the packages that showed up at your door. Most people can't answer that question without guessing. And the guess is almost always lower than the truth.

That's what this book is about. It isn't a lecture, and I'm not here to judge you. I'm here to help you do the math you've been avoiding, and then show you what that money could become if it had a different job.

This book is written for the person who can afford their habits. The bills get paid. The paycheck keeps coming. The smoke, the drinks, the bets, and the shopping don't hurt in the moment. That's exactly why the money slips away: nothing ever forces you to stop. So the question was never "can I afford it?" The question is "what else could that money be doing?"

In the pages ahead, we'll look at what your habits really cost, why they stick, and how to loosen their grip without turning your life upside down. Then we'll talk about where that money can go instead: Bitcoin, Ethereum, and the world of decentralized finance, which people call DeFi. It's a new way of handling money, and it's growing fast.

A few things before we start.

You don't have to quit everything. This isn't about being perfect. It's about being intentional, and even a small shift makes a real difference over time.

Nothing in this book is a promise. Crypto moves up and down, sometimes hard, and I'll be straight with you about the risks. I'll show you how to start small, protect yourself, and keep from getting got. This is education, not financial advice, and every choice is yours.

Get high or get by. Pick your lane. Let's get into it.

CHAPTER 1

Everybody's Got One

Think about your Friday. The check lands around noon, and by one o'clock you already have a plan. Maybe it's the spot where the bartender knows your order. Maybe it's the stop on the way home, or the drive-thru after a long week, or the cart you've been "just looking at" since Tuesday. Nobody forced you. Nobody even had to ask. The plan formed on its own, the way it always does, and the money moved.

That's a vice. It's anything you keep paying for that takes more from you than it gives back. It could be the smoke, the drinks, the fast food, the scratch-offs, the sports bets, the sneakers you already have three pairs of, or the fourteen subscriptions you forgot to cancel.

And I want to be clear about something: none of this makes you a bad person. Most vices started out as a way to relax, celebrate, or get through a rough day. Somebody handed you a drink at a good time in your life, or a long week made the drive-thru feel earned. That's human. The trouble starts when the thing stops being an occasion and turns into a routine. Anything you repeat with money attached eventually becomes a bill.

Why it sticks

A vice isn't a mystery. It sticks because it works, at least for a minute. The smoke takes the edge off. The drinks make a long week feel shorter. The food hits at eleven at night when you're tired and don't feel like cooking. The package arriving gives you a small rush, and for a second the day feels better. Your brain remembers that relief. The next time the same feeling shows up, it points you right back to the same fix.

That's the loop: a trigger shows up, you act on the habit, you get the reward, and then you repeat it. The more times you run that loop, the more automatic it gets, until you're not choosing anymore. You're just following the groove.

There's a crowd factor too. A lot of vices live in a social setting: the cookout, the lounge, the group chat, the Sunday game. Sometimes the habit isn't really about the thing itself. It's about who you're with when you do it. That makes it harder to spot, and harder to change.

The trap of "I can afford it"

Here's what makes this different when you're doing okay. When money is tight, a habit shows itself fast. The cash runs out and you feel it. But when you're stable, nothing pushes back. The rent gets paid. The car note gets paid. The habit gets paid too. There's no alarm and no warning light.

So the habit doesn't feel expensive. It feels normal, like it's part of the budget, part of life. And that's the trap. When nothing hurts, nothing changes. Meanwhile the money goes out week after week, and a few years later you look up and wonder where it all went.

I'm not telling you to feel bad about it. You earned that money. I'm asking you to look at it the way a business owner would look at an expense: is this getting me something I would choose again?

What the money isn't doing

Every dollar you spend is a dollar that can't do something else. Economists call that opportunity cost, and it's the quiet part of every vice.

When you spend a hundred dollars on something that's gone by Sunday, you're not only out the hundred. You're out whatever that hundred could have become if you'd put it to work. One time, it doesn't matter. But when it's the same hundred every week for years, that's a lot of building you never did.

That's the whole idea behind this book. You don't have to give up your whole life. You just have to give some of that money a better job.

But first, we need to know the real number. That's next.

CHAPTER 2

Do the Math

Let's start with the one everybody knows. I'm not talking about the average backyard boogie. I'm talking about the good stuff, the Kush and the Za, the top shelf. Prices change depending on where you live and what you're buying, so just for argument's sake, let's say it's ten dollars a gram.

Someone smoking an eighth a day, 3.5 grams, is spending thirty-five dollars a day. That's $245 a week. It's $1,085 in a 31-day month. And over a year, it comes out to $12,775.

Now say you're smoking seven grams a day. That's a seventy-dollar habit, and it runs you $25,550 a year. If your intake is higher than that, or your prices are, do the math with your own numbers. It only goes one direction.

And that's before the extras. The lighters, the papers, the bowls, the grinders, the cigars, the new piece you buy after you drop the old one. All of that raises the total, and even a portion of it could have been redirected toward something that builds.

So let's flip it. Say you cut back just three days out of every seven, and on those days you put the money toward a little Bitcoin or Ethereum instead. For the daily eighth smoker at ten dollars a gram, that's $105 a week, or $5,460 a year. You can burn it or you can build with it. The choice is yours.

The same math, other habits

Weed isn't the only one that adds up. Here's how a few other common habits look over a year. These are examples, so swap in your own prices and see where you land.

Habit What I assumed Per week Per year
Weed (an eighth a day) $35 a day $245 $12,775
Cigarettes $10 a day $70 $3,650
Fast food $15 a day $105 $5,475
Drinking $10 a drink, 5 a week $50 $2,600
Impulse buys $50 a week $50 $2,600
Sports bets and scratch-offs $40 a week $40 $2,080

You don't have to be extreme for this to add up. Even ten dollars a week is $520 a year.

Where the extra money hides

The sticker price is never the whole price. Most habits have costs hiding around the edges, and those are the ones people forget to count.

Take fast food. A fifteen-dollar meal turns into twenty-five once you add the delivery fee, the service fee, and the tip. Do that four times a week and you're spending $100 a week, or $5,200 a year, on food that showed up in a paper bag. Drinking works the same way. A ten-dollar drink is really twelve with the tip, and that's before the cover charge, the bottle, the ride home, and the brunch the next morning to recover. By the time the night is over, the drinks were the smallest part of the bill.

Betting is sneakier. The tickets and the parlays are cheap one at a time, which is exactly why nobody tracks them. A five here and a twenty there doesn't feel like anything, until you add it up and the total shocks you. Shopping hides it differently, in shipping and returns you never make and the matching outfit you bought for the thing you bought. And then there are the subscriptions: the streaming service you forgot to cancel, the app you never open, the gym you haven't visited since spring. Pull up your bank statement and count them. Most people find at least three.

The ten-year view

Here's another way to look at it. Pick a number you could redirect each week and let it run.

Put away twenty-five dollars a week and you've contributed $1,300 in a year and $13,000 in ten. Make it fifty dollars a week, and that's $2,600 a year and $26,000 in ten. At a hundred a week, it's $5,200 a year and $52,000 over ten years. At two-fifty a week, you're looking at $13,000 a year and $130,000 over a decade.

Notice that those are only the dollars you put in. They don't include any growth, and they don't account for any losses either. That part depends on the market, and we'll get to it. But look at that hundred-a-week number again. It's less than what the daily eighth smoker spends in three days. Nobody has to give up everything for this. You just pick a number and stay consistent.

And here's the part people miss: you're going to spend that money one way or the other. The only question is whether, ten years from now, you have something to show for it.

FROM CHAPTER 8: TAKING YOUR POWER BACK

Three people, three habits, one plan

Before we go further, here's what this looks like in real life. A quick note: the three people you're about to meet are composites. They're made-up examples, built from common situations, not real individuals. All three earn enough to afford their habits, which is the whole point. The numbers are just math, and they don't promise anything about how an investment will perform.

Tre was an eighth-a-day guy. Thirty-five dollars a day, every day, and he never thought twice about it because the bills were handled. One night he added it up on his phone and stared at the screen for a while. He didn't quit cold turkey. He cut back three days a week and filled those evenings at the gym. The $105 a week he wasn't spending, he moved into Bitcoin every Friday before he could talk himself out of it. After a year, he'd put $5,460 to work. What it was worth depended on the market, but the bigger change was the habit itself. He'd started paying himself first.

Nia lived on fast food. About every day, around fifteen dollars a meal, which came to nearly $5,475 a year. She wasn't struggling. She just never had time to cook, or so she thought. She started meal prepping on Sundays, and she cut the takeout down to a few times a week. Her grocery bill went up a little, but she still redirected about fifty dollars a week, which is $2,600 a year. The thing she mentioned most was how much better she felt in her own body.

Mike put about forty dollars a week into sports bets and scratch-offs, around $2,080 a year. He didn't win it back, because most people don't. When he moved that forty dollars into a monthly buy of Bitcoin and Ethereum, he said the difference was simple: the money stayed his instead of going to the house.

None of them were perfect. Each one cut a little, replaced the habit with something else, and moved the money on a schedule. That's the whole play.

FROM CHAPTER 7: THE CART

The 48-hour rule, and other ways to take control

Start with an audit. Pull up your bank and card statements for the last three months and highlight every recurring charge. Cancel what you don't use. Do it today, while the list is in front of you. For a lot of people, this single hour recovers more money than any other step in the book.

Then put in some friction. Delete saved payment info from the shopping sites you use most. Unsubscribe from the promotional emails and turn off the sale notifications. Remove the apps from your phone and use the website, which is a little harder. Every extra step gives your brain a chance to ask a question.

That leads to the best trick in this chapter, which is the 48-hour rule. When you want something that isn't a necessity, put it on a list and wait two days. If you still want it after two days, and you can afford it, buy it. You'll find that a surprising number of things lose their shine by Thursday.

For bigger purchases, try the cost-per-use test. A jacket you'll wear a hundred times for $200 is two dollars a wear. A pair of shoes you wear twice is a hundred dollars a wear. The question isn't whether it's expensive. It's whether you'll actually use it.

FROM CHAPTER 10: YOUR MONEY MIND

Fear of missing out, and what to do about it

FOMO is the most expensive feeling in investing. You see a chart going up, or a post from someone showing their gains, or a friend who's suddenly talking about a coin you've never heard of. Your stomach tightens. You feel like the train is leaving, and you're standing on the platform.

That feeling is real, and it's also a terrible advisor. Prices that are shooting up have usually already moved. By the time something is all over your feed, a lot of the early money has already been made. The people shouting about it aren't doing you a favor. Some of them are looking for someone to buy what they're holding.

The antidote is a schedule. If you've already decided to buy a set amount every Friday, you don't need to make a decision when the excitement hits. You've already made it. The plan does the work, and you get to skip the drama.

FROM CHAPTER 11: CRYPTO, EXPLAINED

Meme coins: the lottery ticket in disguise

One group of those other coins needs its own warning, because it's the one most likely to catch your eye. Meme coins are tokens built around a joke, a cartoon animal, a slogan, or a famous name. They usually don't do anything. There's no product behind them, no business, no use. Their value comes down to one thing: how many people are paying attention right now.

Anyone can create one in minutes, for almost nothing. That's why there are so many, and why new ones appear every day. Some get a rush of attention and shoot up by huge amounts in hours. You'll hear stories of people turning a few hundred dollars into a fortune. What you don't hear are the stories of everybody who bought in after the rush and rode it back down. When the attention moves on, the price often falls to almost nothing, and most never come back.

There are a few ways people get hurt. The creators and early insiders often hold a big share of the supply, so when the price pops, they sell into the excitement and leave everyone else holding the bag. That's called a pump and dump. Some are rug pulls from the start, built to take your money and vanish. Some are pushed by influencers or celebrities who were paid to hype them, or who got in early and want exit liquidity. Sometimes there are so few buyers that you can't sell at all, even if the screen says you're up. And scammers love to clone the name of a famous token, so you can end up buying a fake.

Underneath all of it is a feeling this book has been warning you about. It's the rush of the bet, the hope that this one is the one, the fear of missing out. If that sounds familiar, it should. A meme coin is closer to a scratch-off or a parlay than it is to an investment. If you cut the sports bets out of your life and started throwing the same money at meme coins, you didn't redirect a habit. You swapped one vice for another and gave it a more modern name.

If you're curious anyway, treat it as entertainment money, not investment money. That means an amount small enough that you'd shrug if it went to zero, kept completely separate from your plan, and never funded from your emergency savings or borrowed. Don't chase one because it's trending, because someone in a group chat swears by it, or because a stranger sent you a link. By the time you hear about it, the early money has usually already been made.

The plan in this book is the boring one on purpose. Bitcoin and Ethereum, bought on a schedule, held for years. Boring is how you stay in the game.
